Students generalize from the examples they see. If every correct example shares an irrelevant feature, that feature can become part of the learner's rule. If two confusable cases are never compared, the learner may never notice the difference.

Example design therefore determines what is taught, not just how practice looks.

## Reveal sameness with varied examples

To teach what different cases have in common, Crush AP varies the scenario while holding the important wording and operation steady.

For example, a model set may change the object, direction, numbers, and diagram while preserving the same problem-solving routine. The variation makes the shared structure visible.

## Reveal differences with minimal pairs

To teach a distinction, change one important feature at a time and name it directly.

In *Connecting Math Concepts*, students distinguish the slanted side length of a parallelogram from its perpendicular height before calculating area. The figures are deliberately similar. The questions focus attention on the one feature that changes the answer.

The same method applies in physics:

- positive and negative direction;
- velocity versus acceleration;
- a force that acts on an object versus a force the object exerts;
- a graph's value versus its slope;
- a quantity that is given versus a quantity that must be inferred.

## Teach the case space in an intentional order

<Steps>
  <Step title="Start with the cleanest positive case">
    Attach the new term, rule, or routine to an example that makes its defining feature easy to see.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Test a nearby non-example">
    Introduce a tempting confusion quickly. Ask the student to use the rule to reject it.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Build a mixed discrimination set">
    Mix positives and negatives so position, wording, or interface patterns cannot become a shortcut.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Move from identification to application">
    Once the distinction is firm, use it inside calculations, explanations, diagrams, and exam-style problems.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Sequence new work from established work

New content should lean on what the student has already produced, not on what an author assumes they probably know. Prerequisites are reactivated just before use through a short response whenever possible.

Multi-step routines are built in the same way: teach a step, check it, add the next step, check it, then run the complete routine with less support.

<Info>
  A large bank of examples is not automatically a good example set. The cases must cover the intended universe without introducing accidental clues or unnecessary distinctions.
</Info>
